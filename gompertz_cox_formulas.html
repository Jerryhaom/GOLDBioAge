GOLD and Cox BioAge: Derivation and R Examples

Cox derivation · R fitting example · Full Cox tutorial

Based on the framework by Hao et al. (Advanced Science) – a proportional hazards approach to transform clinical biomarkers into a mortality‑calibrated age measure.

1. Gompertz model for mortality

The Gompertz distribution is widely used to model adult mortality. Its hazard function increases exponentially with age, capturing the accelerating risk of death. The two‑parameter Gompertz hazard is:

\[ h(t \mid a,b) = b e^{a t} \]

where \(a\) (shape) describes the rate of risk acceleration and \(b\) (rate) scales the baseline hazard. The corresponding cumulative hazard and survival functions are:

\[ H(t) = \frac{b}{a}(e^{a t} - 1), \qquad S(t) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{b}{a}(e^{a t} - 1)\right). \]

2. Proportional hazards formulation with covariates

In a proportional hazards framework, covariates shift the hazard multiplicatively. For an individual with chronological age (CA) and a set of biomarkers, the Gompertz hazard becomes:

\[ h(t \mid \text{CA}, \mathbf{X}) = b \exp\!\left(\beta_{\text{age}}\cdot\text{CA} + \sum \beta_i X_i + a t\right). \]

The parameter \(a\) is assumed constant across the population, meaning that age and biomarkers affect only the level of risk, not the rate of its increase with time.

3. Core derivation of GOLD BioAge

The GOLD BioAge is defined by matching the instantaneous hazard of an individual (with observed age and biomarkers) to the hazard of a reference individual whose only known attribute is a hypothetical “biological age”. Two nested Gompertz models are fitted:

Model 1 (chronological age only):

\[ h_1(t, \text{age}) = b_1 \exp\!\left(a_1 t + \beta_1 \cdot \text{age}\right) \]

Model 2 (age + biomarkers):

\[ h_2(t, \text{age}, \mathbf{X}) = b_2 \exp\!\left(a_2 t + \beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \sum \beta_i X_i\right) \]

At \(t = 0\) (the time origin of the survival analysis), the hazard of an individual in Model 2 is set equal to the hazard of a person in Model 1 whose age equals the GOLD BioAge:

\[ h_1(t=0,\; \text{BioAge}) = h_2(t=0,\; \text{age}, \mathbf{X}). \]

Substituting the expressions gives:

\[ b_1 e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{BioAge}} = b_2 \exp\!\left(\beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \sum \beta_i X_i\right). \]

Solving for BioAge yields:

\[ \text{BioAge} = \frac{1}{\beta_1}\left(\beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \sum \beta_i X_i + \ln\frac{b_2}{b_1}\right). \]

3.1 Bias correction via a scaling factor \(\gamma\)

In practice, the two models may have slight differences in baseline hazard even when the same covariates are used. To account for this, a correction factor \(\gamma\) is introduced such that \(h_1 = \gamma \cdot h_2\) at the reference level. The hazard equality then becomes:

\[ b_1 e^{\beta_1 \cdot \text{BioAge}} = \gamma \cdot b_2 \exp\!\left(\beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \sum \beta_i X_i\right), \]

leading to:

\[ \text{BioAge} = \frac{1}{\beta_1}\left(\beta_2 \cdot \text{age} + \sum \beta_i X_i + \ln\frac{\gamma b_2}{b_1}\right). \]

To keep the interpretation simple, the coefficient for chronological age is often constrained to be equal across models (\(\beta_2 = \beta_1\)). The final formula becomes:

\[ \boxed{\text{GOLD BioAge} = \text{age} + \frac{1}{\beta_1}\sum \beta_i X_i + \frac{1}{\beta_1}\ln\frac{\gamma b_2}{b_1}}. \]

The package defines a single training-sample correction using \(\ln\gamma=\operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{train}}[\ln\{h_1(0\mid A)/h_2(0\mid A,X)\}]\). Together with the fixed age effect, this makes the intercept equal to \(b_0=\operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{train}}(A-s)\), where \(s=A+\sum_i\beta_iX_i/\beta_1\). Mean training GOLD BioAge therefore equals mean training chronological age by construction. In the bundled NHANES fit, the GOLD intercept is approximately -53.62873289 years; it is not a universal +4.7-year correction. Keep the fitted intercept fixed when scoring new data.

4. Cox-derived biological age

The same conversion of biomarker effects to age units can be used with Cox proportional-hazards regression. First fit an age-only reference model, then fix its age effect through an offset in a model containing the biomarkers:

\[ h_1(t\mid A)=h_{01}(t)e^{\beta_A A},\qquad h_2(t\mid A,X)=h_{02}(t)\exp\left(\beta_A A+\sum_j\theta_jX_j\right). \]

The Cox baseline hazards are unspecified and need not be equal. Define the uncalibrated age score and one intercept on the complete-case training sample:

\[ s_i=A_i+\sum_j\frac{\theta_j}{\beta_A}X_{ij},\qquad b_0=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\sum_{i\in\mathrm{train}}(A_i-s_i). \] \[ \boxed{\mathrm{CoxBioAge}_i=s_i+b_0.} \]

Equivalently, \(b_0=\operatorname{mean}_{\mathrm{train}}(\ell_1^0-\ell_2^0)/\beta_A\), where \(\ell_1^0=\beta_A A\) and \(\ell_2^0=\beta_A A+\sum_j\theta_jX_j\) are uncentered linear predictors. The reference age coefficient must be nonzero. The age effect becomes 1 after conversion to years, and mean training Cox BioAge equals mean training age. A different population need not have a zero mean score difference; retain the training intercept during transfer.

This is a calibrated relative-risk-derived age score. Absolute hazard equivalence at a chosen follow-up time would additionally involve \(h_{02}(t)/h_{01}(t)\), so it cannot in general be inferred by cancelling the two Cox baseline hazards. This function does not directly estimate an absolute mortality probability.

4.1 Implementation and calibration convention

cox_bioage() fits both Cox models with Efron handling of tied event times. The full model fixes age through an offset; the estimated biomarker effects are divided by the age-only coefficient. The original weight matrix remains in fit$coefficients; the complete portable vector, including beta0, is in fit$score_coefficients. Use predict_cox_bioage() for new observations.

Version 0.2.1 calibration change: default predict.coxph(type = "lp") values are centered, and offsets follow a different convention from ordinary covariates. Older package code combined those predictions with raw age and biomarker weights. The default now calculates mean(age - linear_score) directly. Fitted weights are unchanged, but score levels can change. Use calibration = "legacy" to reproduce the intercept and scores from version 0.2.0 or earlier; saved older objects keep their stored intercept when passed to predict_cox_bioage().

On the bundled NHANES complete-case sample (39,348 participants, 4,716 deaths), the default Cox intercept is approximately -53.56354467. Mean calibrated Cox BioAge and mean age are both approximately 49.49797 years. The legacy intercept is approximately -49.49796686 and gives a mean score of 53.56354 years. These are computed examples, not evidence of independent clinical validation.

5. Cox screening versus a final Cox model

In the current R package, gold_bioage(feature_selection = TRUE) uses a biomarker-only penalized Cox model to select predictors, then fits a final Gompertz model with age retained. The implemented selector uses lambda.min, with LASSO or elastic net and a configurable fold count and seed. It does not automatically perform bootstrap stability assessment. This screening step is different from calling cox_bioage() to fit the final Cox-derived score.

Perform feature selection and preprocessing inside the development sample. The full GOLD example uses nine biomarkers plus age: creatinine, glucose, MCV, RDW, albumin, ALP, lymphocyte percentage, WBC, and GGT. See the original article for its research analyses and the package tutorials for the currently executable workflow.

6. Derivation of Levine’s Phenotypic Age

Levine’s Phenotypic Age [1] is also grounded in the Gompertz model but uses the cumulative distribution function to match 10‑year mortality risk. Two Gompertz proportional hazards models are constructed:

Model A (only chronological age):

\[ F_1(t=120, \text{PhenoAge}) = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\exp(\hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot \text{PhenoAge}) \cdot \frac{e^{120 \hat{a}} - 1}{\hat{a}}\right) \]

Model B (age + biomarkers):

\[ F_2(t=120, \text{age}, \mathbf{X}) = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\exp(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \cdot \frac{e^{120 \hat{a}} - 1}{\hat{a}}\right) \]

where \(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} = \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_{\text{age}}\cdot\text{age} + \sum \hat{\beta}_i X_i\). The time horizon \(t=120\) months corresponds to 10 years.

The underlying idea is to find the hypothetical age (PhenoAge) such that the 10‑year mortality risk predicted by Model A (using only that age) equals the risk predicted by Model B (using actual age and biomarkers). Thus we set:

\[ F_1(t=120, \text{PhenoAge}) = F_2(t=120, \text{age}, \mathbf{X}). \]

Substituting the cumulative distribution expressions gives:

\[ 1 - \exp\!\left(-\exp(\hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot \text{PhenoAge}) \cdot \frac{e^{120 \hat{a}} - 1}{\hat{a}}\right) = 1 - \exp\!\left(-\exp(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}) \cdot \frac{e^{120 \hat{a}} - 1}{\hat{a}}\right). \]

Cancelling the outer \(1-\exp(\cdot)\) and equating the inner arguments yields:

\[ \exp(\hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot \text{PhenoAge}) = \exp(\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}). \]

Taking logarithms and solving for PhenoAge:

\[ \hat{\beta}_0 + \hat{\beta}_1 \cdot \text{PhenoAge} = \mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{PhenoAge} = \frac{\mathbf{X}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}} - \hat{\beta}_0}{\hat{\beta}_1}. \]

In practice, the parameters are estimated from the data. Using the NHANES‑derived coefficients, the final formula is expressed as:

\[ \text{Phenotypic Age} = 141.50225 + \frac{\ln\!\bigl(-0.00553 \cdot \ln(1 - F_2(120,X))\bigr)}{0.090165}, \]

where \(F_2(120,X)\) is the predicted 10‑year mortality risk from Model B, and the constants stem from the fitted Gompertz shape and the linear predictor coefficients. This formulation is algebraically equivalent to the linear expression above but is presented in a form that directly incorporates the estimated Gompertz parameters.

While both GOLD BioAge and Levine’s Phenotypic Age rely on the Gompertz law, they differ in the aspect of risk used: GOLD BioAge uses the instantaneous hazard at baseline (\(t=0\)), leading to a simple linear combination of biomarkers; Phenotypic Age uses the 10‑year cumulative risk, which requires solving for the quantile of the cumulative distribution. Both measures are highly correlated and predict mortality similarly, but GOLD BioAge offers greater computational simplicity and direct interpretability as an age‑equivalent mortality rate.

7. Run Gompertz and Cox fitting in R

Install GOLDBioAge 0.2.1 or later using remotes::install_github("Jerryhaom/GOLDBioAge"), then run:

library(GOLDBioAge)
data("NHANES4", package = "GOLDBioAge")
variables <- c("age", "albumin", "alp", "creat", "glucose_mmol",
               "lymph", "mcv", "rdw", "wbc", "ggt")

fit_cox <- cox_bioage(NHANES4, variables)
fit_gold <- gold_bioage(NHANES4, variables)
stopifnot(identical(fit_cox$rows_used, fit_gold$rows_used))

# Predict from biomarkers only; keep the training intercept.
NHANES4$Coxbioage <- predict_cox_bioage(fit_cox, NHANES4[, variables])
fit_cox$score_coefficients
mean(fit_cox$CoxBioage$Coxbioage - fit_cox$CoxBioage$age)

# Compare weights on the same complete cases.
w <- fit_cox$score_coefficients
round(cbind(Gompertz = fit_gold$coefficients[names(w)], Cox = w), 8)


# Reproduce the older intercept convention when needed.
legacy_fit <- cox_bioage(NHANES4, variables, calibration = "legacy")

For a simulated Gompertz cohort with training/test splitting, run the simulated-data script. For the bundled NHANES cohort, run the NHANES script. Both include Cox fitting, fixed-coefficient scoring, and comparison with Gompertz GOLD. Read the full Cox tutorial or the Chinese guide.
